## 1. The Meta-Question: Why Is H36 Interesting Even If Implausible?

The H36 hypothesis operates at the intersection of three frontier research areas that are individually well-funded and actively investigated in 2024-2025:

1. **PBH dark matter** — The asteroid-mass window is the subject of multiple dedicated detection campaigns (Einstein Probe, THESEUS, eXTP). Any result—detection or exclusion—would be transformative.

2. **SMBH environment dynamics** — LISA's primary science target is Extreme Mass Ratio Inspirals (EMRIs) into SMBHs, which directly probe the distribution of compact objects in the inner parsec. If PBHs exist there, LISA could detect them.

3. **Gravitational wave astrophysics** — PBH-PBH mergers in the mini-spike around Sgr A* would produce a stochastic GW background detectable by pulsar timing arrays (NANOGrav).

H36 is therefore not "wild speculation divorced from observable reality" but rather a **synthesis hypothesis** that connects three independently testable research programs. Even the failure of any one component constrains the others.

## 2. Deep Reasoning: Six Arguments For and Against

### FOR H36 (The Steel-Man Case)

#### Argument F1: "The Mini-Spike Is Basically Guaranteed"
If PBHs exist in any mass range, they **must** accumulate around SMBHs. Dynamical friction, gravitational focusing, and adiabatic growth of the SMBH potential well all conspire to create a steep density spike. Gondolo & Silk (1999) showed this for generic CDM; Eroshenko (2024) confirmed it specifically for PBHs. The spike forms passively — no exotic physics needed. The only question is whether PBHs exist at all.

**Counter:** True, but the spike also depletes via PBH-PBH mergers, stellar disruption, and SMBH accretion. The steady-state spike mass may be much lower than the naive adiabatic prediction.

#### Argument F2: "IMBH Inspirals Are Expected and Would Disrupt the Spike"
N-body simulations of nuclear star clusters predict that $10^3$–$10^4\,M_\odot$ IMBHs should form and inspiral toward Sgr A* on $\sim 10^9$ year timescales. Each IMBH inspiral would gravitationally scatter PBHs out of the mini-spike, creating a periodic "PBH ejection event." The Milky Way has had multiple such events over its history.

**Counter:** The IMBH inspiral timescale is much longer than the PBH spike refilling timescale via dynamical friction, so each ejection event may only partially disrupt the spike.

#### Argument F3: "We Already Know PBHs Pass Through the Solar System"
Even without any exotic ejection mechanism, if PBHs constitute even $\sim 1\%$ of dark matter at $10^{20}$ g, $\sim 30$ PBHs per year pass within Earth's orbit. This is not speculation — it's a direct consequence of the local DM density and kinematics. The only "insane" part of H36 is the *enhanced flux* from a catastrophic ejection.

**Counter:** The ambient rate is interesting for detection campaigns but produces no measurable effects on the solar system.

### AGAINST H36 (The Honest Critique)

#### Argument A1: "The $d^2$ Problem Is Fatal for Any 'Shotgun' Scenario"
The $1/d^2$ flux dilution over $8.2$ kpc ($2.5 \times 10^{22}$ cm) is $\sim 10^{-45}$ cm$^{-2}$ per source. No physically plausible ejection event can overcome this geometric dilution to produce a detectable concentrated "blast" at the solar system. The metaphor of a "shotgun" is misleading — it's more like a "shotgun fired into the Pacific Ocean from the Moon."

**Counter (partial):** Beamed ejection along the jet axis could improve the factor by $\sim 10^3$, but this introduces the requirement that the jet points at us (also very specific).

#### Argument A2: "Dynamical Friction Heating Is $10^{15}$ Orders of Magnitude Too Weak"
The energy deposited by a $10^{20}$-g PBH transit through Earth is $\sim 3$ tons of TNT — spread through an atomic-scale tube through the entire planet. This is thermodynamically meaningless. To produce a "heating flash" visible from space, you'd need a PBH of $\sim 10$ Earth masses, which is excluded by microlensing.

**Counter:** The simulation can dramatically amplify the effect for visual purposes without claiming physical accuracy. Many other simulation hypotheses (H33, H34, H37, H38) already do this.

#### Argument A3: "The No-Hair Theorem Constrains the 'Core Isn't Monolithic' Claim"
Once PBHs cross the event horizon of Sgr A*, they are absorbed. The "swarm inside the SMBH" interpretation is only valid if the swarm orbits *outside* the event horizon. This is perfectly fine physically — the mini-spike is outside — but the hypothesis text implies the SMBH *itself* is composed of a swarm, which is a meaningful distinction.

**Counter:** This is a semantic issue. The physically correct statement is: "Sgr A*'s near-horizon environment contains a dense swarm of orbiting PBHs in addition to the central SMBH." This is the standard mini-spike picture and is entirely consistent with GR.

## 3. Novel Theoretical Extensions

### 3.1 The Binary PBH Population

If asteroid-mass PBHs formed in the early universe, a fraction would have formed as **gravitationally bound binaries** (Nakamura et al. 1997, Sasaki et al. 2016). The binary fraction depends on the PBH mass function and initial spatial clustering but could be $\sim 1$–$10\%$.

**Novel prediction:** When the mini-spike is disrupted, ejected PBH binaries would merge during or after ejection (due to GW inspiral accelerated by the ejection-induced eccentricity). These mergers would produce a **burst of high-frequency gravitational waves** at:

$$f_{\text{GW}} = \frac{c^3}{G M_{\text{binary}}} \sim \frac{3 \times 10^{10}}{6.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 2 \times 10^{20}} \sim 2 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{Hz}^{-1}$$

Wait — let me recalculate. The GW frequency at merger (ISCO) for total mass $M$:

$$f_{\text{ISCO}} = \frac{c^3}{6^{3/2} \pi G M} = \frac{(3\times10^{10})^3}{6^{3/2} \pi \times 6.67\times10^{-8} \times 2\times10^{20}}$$

$$= \frac{2.7 \times 10^{31}}{14.7 \times \pi \times 1.33 \times 10^{13}} = \frac{2.7 \times 10^{31}}{6.16 \times 10^{14}} \approx 4.4 \times 10^{16}\,\text{Hz}$$

This is in the **ultraviolet/X-ray frequency range!** These would be undetectable by any GW detector (LIGO: $10$–$10^4$ Hz; LISA: $10^{-4}$–$10^{-1}$ Hz). However, they would contribute to a stochastic high-frequency GW background that is currently unconstrained.

### 3.2 PBH Accretion During Solar Transit

A PBH transiting through the Sun would accrete matter at the Bondi-Hoyle rate:

$$\dot{M}_{\text{BH}} = \frac{4\pi G^2 M_{\text{PBH}}^2 \rho_\odot}{(v_{\text{PBH}}^2 + c_s^2)^{3/2}}$$

where $c_s \sim 5 \times 10^7$ cm/s is the sound speed in the solar core.

For $M_{\text{PBH}} = 10^{20}$ g, $v_{\text{PBH}} = 0.99c \gg c_s$:

$$\dot{M} = \frac{4\pi (6.67 \times 10^{-8})^2 (10^{20})^2 \times 150}{(3 \times 10^{10})^3} = \frac{4\pi \times 4.45 \times 10^{-15} \times 10^{40} \times 150}{2.7 \times 10^{31}}$$

$$= \frac{4\pi \times 6.67 \times 10^{27}}{2.7 \times 10^{31}} = \frac{8.38 \times 10^{28}}{2.7 \times 10^{31}} \approx 3.1 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{g/s}$$

Transit time through the Sun: $t = 2R_\odot / v \approx 1.4 \times 10^{11} / 3 \times 10^{10} \approx 4.7$ s.

Total mass accreted: $\Delta M \approx 3.1 \times 10^{-3} \times 4.7 \approx 0.015$ g.

This is **negligible** — the PBH gains $\sim 10^{-20}$ of its own mass during solar transit. ❌

### 3.3 Hawking Radiation from the PBH Swarm

Each PBH emits Hawking radiation with luminosity:

$$L_H = \frac{\hbar c^6}{15360 \pi G^2 M^2}$$

For $M = 10^{20}$ g:

$$L_H = \frac{1.055 \times 10^{-27} \times (3 \times 10^{10})^6}{15360 \pi \times (6.67 \times 10^{-8})^2 \times (10^{20})^2}$$

$$= \frac{1.055 \times 10^{-27} \times 7.29 \times 10^{62}}{15360 \pi \times 4.45 \times 10^{-15} \times 10^{40}}$$

$$= \frac{7.69 \times 10^{35}}{2.15 \times 10^{30}} \approx 3.6 \times 10^{5}\,\text{erg/s}$$

$$\approx 3.6 \times 10^{-2}\,\text{W}$$

Each PBH glows with $\sim 36$ milliwatts of Hawking radiation. For a swarm of $10^{10}$ PBHs:

$$L_{\text{total}} = 10^{10} \times 0.036 \approx 3.6 \times 10^{8}\,\text{W} = 360\,\text{MW}$$

This is significant but at Hawking temperature:

$$T_H = \frac{\hbar c^3}{8\pi G M k_B} = \frac{1.055 \times 10^{-27} \times 2.7 \times 10^{31}}{8\pi \times 6.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 10^{20} \times 1.38 \times 10^{-16}}$$

$$\approx \frac{2.85 \times 10^{4}}{2.31 \times 10^{-2}} \approx 1.2 \times 10^{6}\,\text{K}$$

At $\sim 10^6$ K, the Hawking radiation peaks in the **X-ray band** ($\lambda \sim 3$ nm). A swarm of $10^{10}$ PBHs would glow collectively in X-rays at $\sim 360$ MW = $3.6 \times 10^9$ erg/s. This is $\sim 10^{-24} L_\odot$ — entirely undetectable. ❌

**However**, for smaller PBHs near the evaporation boundary ($M \sim 10^{15}$–$10^{16}$ g), the Hawking temperature reaches $\sim 10^{11}$ K (gamma-ray regime) and the luminosity becomes significant. These would be detectable — and **have been searched for** by Fermi-LAT and INTEGRAL. Non-detection constrains $f_{\text{PBH}}$ at this mass scale.

### 3.4 The Relativistic Beaming Enhancement

A PBH ejected at $\gamma = 7$ ($v = 0.99c$) has its Hawking radiation **relativistically beamed** in the forward direction. An observer in the PBH's path sees the Hawking luminosity boosted by:

$$L_{\text{obs}} = \delta^4 \times L_H$$

where the Doppler factor $\delta = \gamma(1 - \beta\cos\theta)^{-1}$. For head-on approach ($\theta = 0$):

$$\delta = \frac{1}{\gamma(1 - \beta)} = \frac{1}{7 \times 0.01} = \frac{1}{0.07} \approx 14.3$$

$$L_{\text{obs}} = 14.3^4 \times 0.036\,\text{W} = 41,800 \times 0.036 \approx 1500\,\text{W}$$

And the Hawking temperature is blue-shifted:

$$T_{\text{obs}} = \delta \times T_H = 14.3 \times 1.2 \times 10^6 \approx 1.7 \times 10^7\,\text{K}$$

Now in the **hard X-ray** band. 1500 W from a point source — still far too faint for detection at astronomical distances, but theoretically interesting. A sufficiently sensitive detector in the direct path of a relativistic PBH might see a brief, hard X-ray flash.

## 4. Quantum Gravity Connections (Truly Speculative)

### 4.1 Information Paradox Implications

If the PBH mini-spike around Sgr A* contains $10^{20}$ PBHs, each carrying quantum information on their stretched (scrambled) horizon, the total **holographic information content** of the mini-spike is:

$$I_{\text{total}} = N \times \frac{A}{4 l_P^2}$$

where $A = 4\pi r_s^2$ is the horizon area of each PBH and $l_P = 1.6 \times 10^{-33}$ cm is the Planck length.

For $M = 10^{20}$ g ($r_s = 1.5 \times 10^{-8}$ cm):

$$\frac{A}{4l_P^2} = \frac{4\pi (1.5 \times 10^{-8})^2}{4 \times (1.6 \times 10^{-33})^2} = \frac{2.83 \times 10^{-15}}{1.02 \times 10^{-65}} \approx 2.8 \times 10^{50}\,\text{bits}$$

Total for the swarm: $I_{\text{total}} \sim 10^{20} \times 10^{50} = 10^{70}$ bits.

For comparison, the Sgr A* SMBH itself ($M = 4 \times 10^6\,M_\odot$, $r_s \approx 1.2 \times 10^{12}$ cm):

$$\frac{A_{\text{SMBH}}}{4l_P^2} = \frac{4\pi (1.2 \times 10^{12})^2}{1.02 \times 10^{-65}} \approx \frac{1.8 \times 10^{25}}{10^{-65}} \approx 1.8 \times 10^{90}\,\text{bits}$$

The PBH swarm carries $10^{70}/10^{90} = 10^{-20}$ of the SMBH's information — negligible in total, but distributed across $10^{20}$ independent horizons. Each PBH is an **independent quantum system** — unlike the monolithic SMBH, the swarm does not scramble information across all its members.

**Speculative implication:** If the PBH swarm is ejected, each PBH carries its quantum information **intact** (modulo slow Hawking evaporation). The ejection event is, in a sense, a **partial information extraction** from the SMBH's environment — not from inside the SMBH (which would violate no-hair), but from its gravitational domain.

### 4.2 Connection to H34 (Holographic Pixelation Shock)

The Bekenstein bound limits the maximum information in a region of space:

$$I_{\max} = \frac{2\pi R E}{\hbar c \ln 2}$$

A massive PBH transiting through a region momentarily increases the local $E$ (by its relativistic kinetic energy $\gamma M c^2$), raising the local Bekenstein bound. This is the **opposite** of H34's "pixelation" — the PBH transit briefly **increases** the information capacity of local space.

However, if $N$ PBHs transit simultaneously, the cumulative energy density could (in the simulation's speculative mode) be said to "stress" the holographic boundary — connecting H36 to H34 through a novel pathway not previously considered.

## 5. The Revised H36: A Three-Tier Hierarchy

Based on this analysis, I recommend restructuring H36 into three tiers of decreasing plausibility:

### Tier 1: Ambient PBH Transit (HIGH Confidence)
- **What:** PBH dark matter particles drift through the solar system at galactic orbital velocities (~200 km/s)
- **Rate:** ~3 per year within 1 AU (if $f_{\text{PBH}} = 1$ at $10^{20}$ g)
- **Effect:** Undetectable with current technology; future seismic arrays may detect Earth transits
- **Theory Status:** Standard dark matter physics; awaiting observational confirmation of PBH existence

### Tier 2: Enhanced PBH Flux from Mini-Spike Disruption (MODERATE Confidence)  
- **What:** An IMBH inspiral into Sgr A* disrupts the PBH mini-spike, gradually dispersing excess PBHs into the galactic halo
- **Rate enhancement:** Local PBH density increases by $10^2$–$10^5$ over $\sim 10^7$ years
- **Effect:** Subtle increase in Oort Cloud perturbation rate; potential signature in comet injection statistics
- **Theory Status:** Consistent with GR and CDM dynamics; testable with LISA (IMBH detection) and future comet surveys

### Tier 3: The Relativistic "Shotgun" (LOW Confidence — Pure Simulation Spectacle)
- **What:** Catastrophic ejection at $>0.99c$ produces a relativistic PBH "beam"
- **Rate:** Requires cascade of extremely rare events (Penrose + slingshot + SMBH merger)
- **Effect:** At Earth: negligible flux, negligible energy deposition, sub-microsecond micro-lensing
- **Theory Status:** Not forbidden by known physics, but requires simultaneous occurrence of multiple $10^{-10}$-probability events
- **Simulation Value:** ★★★★★ — Spectacular visual regardless of physical plausibility
