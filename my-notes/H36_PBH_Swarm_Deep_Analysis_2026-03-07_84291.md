## Part I: The Hypothesis Restated and Deconstructed

### 1.1 The Core Claim (H36)

> *The SMBH core isn't a single monolithic singularity, but an ultra-dense, localized swarm of Primordial Black Holes (PBHs). A catastrophic destabilization event ejects a fraction of these microscopic, asteroid-mass singularities outward at relativistic speeds (>0.99c).*

This hypothesis contains **four independent sub-claims**, each requiring separate evaluation:

1. **Sub-Claim A:** The interior of Sgr A* contains (or contained) a dense population of PBHs rather than a monolithic singularity.
2. **Sub-Claim B:** These PBHs have asteroid-mass scales ($M \sim 10^{17} - 10^{22}$ g).
3. **Sub-Claim C:** A "catastrophic destabilization event" can eject a fraction of this swarm.
4. **Sub-Claim D:** The ejected PBHs travel at relativistic speeds ($v > 0.99c$, i.e., $\gamma > 7$).

### 1.2 Why This Hypothesis Is Interesting

H36 sits at a unique intersection of:
- **Primordial cosmology** (PBH formation in the early universe)
- **SMBH internal structure** (the information paradox, singularity vs. alternatives)
- **Dark matter physics** (PBHs as DM candidates)
- **Solar system dynamics** (gravitational perturbations from transiting compact objects)
- **Observational astrophysics** (microlensing, seismic detection, orbital wobbles)

It is simultaneously the most **physically grounded** of the H30-H40 "insane" hypotheses (PBHs are a mainstream research topic) and the most **mechanistically challenging** (the ejection mechanism has no established precedent).

## Part II: Sub-Claim Analysis — What Current Physics Says

### 2.1 Sub-Claim A: PBHs Inside/Near an SMBH

#### The Standard Picture
In classical GR, the interior of a Schwarzschild or Kerr black hole is described by the Penrose diagram: a spacelike singularity (Schwarzschild) or a ring singularity (Kerr) lies behind the event horizon. Nothing "orbits" inside. Once past the horizon, all timelike worldlines terminate at the singularity in finite proper time.

**Verdict on "PBHs inside Sgr A*":** Under classical GR, this is **formally impossible**. Anything that crosses the event horizon reaches the singularity. There is no stable internal structure.

#### The Non-Standard Picture — Where It Gets Interesting

However, several active research programs challenge the monolithic singularity picture:

1. **Planck Stars / Loop Quantum Gravity (Rovelli & Vidotto, 2014):** The singularity is replaced by a quantum bounce at Planck density ($\rho_P \sim 5 \times 10^{93}$ g/cm$^3$). The interior could contain a "Planck star" — a maximally compressed object that eventually explodes outward. In this framework, a swarm of PBHs that fell into the SMBH could be in a state of extreme compression without reaching a true singularity.

2. **Fuzzballs (String Theory, Mathur 2005):** The black hole interior is replaced by a stringy, horizon-sized object with no interior volume. PBHs absorbed into a fuzzball would be absorbed into the string state rather than forming a swarm.

3. **Gravastars (Mazur & Mottola, 2004):** The interior of a BH is a de Sitter vacuum (dark energy bubble) bounded by a thin shell at the would-be horizon. PBHs falling in would pile up on or near the shell.

4. **PBH Cluster as SMBH Precursor:** The most physically reasonable interpretation of Sub-Claim A is not that PBHs orbit *inside* the event horizon, but rather that a **dense PBH cluster surrounds or constituted the seed of Sgr A***. This is mainstream:

   - **Carr et al. (2024):** PBHs as seeds for SMBHs via runaway mergers. At $f_{\text{PBH}} \sim 10^{-3}$, PBHs embedded in dense gas can grow to $10^4 - 10^5 M_\odot$ by $z = 20$.
   - **arXiv:2411.03448 (November 2024):** PBHs lose angular momentum via gas dynamical friction, sink into dense cores, form binaries, undergo runaway mergers to create massive seeds.
   - **JWST "little red dots" (2025):** Direct dynamical mass measurements of $\sim 50 \times 10^6 M_\odot$ central masses at $z = 7.04$ strongly favor massive seed models like PBHs.

**Reinterpretation of Sub-Claim A:** Replace "PBHs inside the SMBH" with "a dense PBH population in the central parsec around Sgr A*." This is not only permissible — it is actively supported by Chandra X-ray observations that revealed **thousands of stellar-mass black holes within 3 light-years of Sgr A*** (Hailey et al., 2018, *Nature* 556, 70). Extending this to include a population of *primordial* black holes at lower masses is a natural theoretical extrapolation.

**Feasibility Rating for Sub-Claim A:**
- As stated (PBHs *inside* the event horizon): **1/10** — requires non-standard quantum gravity
- As reinterpreted (PBH cluster in the central parsec): **7/10** — consistent with observations and theory

### 2.3 Sub-Claim C: Ejection Mechanism ("Catastrophic Destabilization")

This is the **weakest and most speculative** sub-claim. Let's examine every plausible ejection mechanism:

#### Mechanism 1: Gravitational Slingshot (Three-Body Ejection)
In a dense cluster of compact objects, three-body interactions can eject the lightest member at high velocity. The typical ejection velocity scales as:

$$v_{\text{ej}} \sim v_{\text{orb}} \sqrt{\frac{M_{\text{binary}}}{M_{\text{ejected}}}}$$

For a PBH of mass $M_{\text{PBH}} \sim 10^{20}$ g $\sim 10^{-13} M_\odot$ interacting with stellar-mass BHs ($M_{\text{BH}} \sim 10 M_\odot$) orbiting at $r \sim 0.01$ pc from Sgr A*:

$$v_{\text{orb}} = \sqrt{\frac{GM_{\text{Sgr A*}}}{r}} = \sqrt{\frac{6.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 8 \times 10^{39}}{3 \times 10^{16}}} \approx 4.2 \times 10^8 \text{ cm/s} \approx 0.014c$$

The mass ratio enhancement: $\sqrt{M_{\text{BH}}/M_{\text{PBH}}} = \sqrt{10 M_\odot / 10^{-13} M_\odot} = \sqrt{10^{14}} \approx 10^7$.

This gives $v_{\text{ej}} \sim 0.014c \times 10^7 \gg c$, which means the energy budget is sufficient but relativistic corrections cap the actual velocity. In practice, three-body ejection in the deep potential well of Sgr A* can **plausibly** accelerate PBHs to mildly relativistic speeds ($v \sim 0.1c - 0.5c$), but reaching $v > 0.99c$ requires extreme fine-tuning.

**Rating: 5/10 for mildly relativistic ejection; 2/10 for >0.99c**

#### Mechanism 2: Gravitational Wave Recoil Kick
When two black holes merge, anisotropic GW emission produces a recoil kick on the remnant. For maximally spinning, unequal-mass mergers:

$$v_{\text{kick}} \sim 5000 \text{ km/s} \approx 0.017c$$

This is well below the $>0.99c$ requirement. Even the most extreme theoretical kicks ($\sim 30,000$ km/s for "superkicks" from aligned-spin configurations) only reach $\sim 0.1c$.

**Rating: 3/10** — Can eject PBHs but not at the required velocities.

#### Mechanism 3: Penrose Process Ejection
A PBH entering the ergosphere of Sgr A* can split its trajectory: part falls in with negative energy, part escapes with enhanced energy. The maximum energy extraction for a single Penrose process event gives:

$$E_{\text{out}} / E_{\text{in}} \leq \frac{1}{2}(1 + \sqrt{2}) \approx 1.207$$

This corresponds to a velocity gain of only ~20%. For repeated Penrose processes (the "collisional" Penrose process), Schnittman (2014) showed that for particles colliding near the ISCO of a maximally spinning BH, the center-of-mass energy can diverge, potentially producing ultra-relativistic ejecta. The escaping particle can theoretically achieve:

$$\gamma_{\text{escape}} \sim \gamma_{\text{CM}}^{1/3}$$

For $a^* \to 1$, $\gamma_{\text{CM}} \to \infty$ (Bañados-Silk-West effect), but astrophysical limitations (finite spin, radiation reaction, geodesic stability) cap $\gamma_{\text{escape}}$ at $\sim 10-100$ in realistic scenarios.

**This is the most promising mechanism for ultra-relativistic ejection.**

**Rating: 6/10** — Theoretically capable of producing $\gamma \sim 7-100$ ejecta, but requires extreme spin ($a^* > 0.998$) and fine-tuned collision geometry.

#### Mechanism 4: Jet Entrainment
PBHs in the polar regions of the accretion flow could be entrained in the relativistic Blandford-Znajek jet. AGN jets achieve $\Gamma \sim 10-50$ (observed in blazars). A PBH embedded in the jet-launching region would be gravitationally dragged along by the jet's energy density via:

$$F_{\text{drag}} \sim \frac{4\pi G^2 M_{\text{PBH}}^2}{c_s^2} \rho_{\text{jet}}$$

However, PBHs interact only gravitationally and are not directly accelerated by electromagnetic forces. The jet is primarily electromagnetic energy (Poynting flux). The gravitational coupling between the jet's energy density and a PBH is:

$$a_{\text{grav}} = \frac{G \cdot (E_{\text{jet}}/c^2)}{r^2}$$

For a jet luminosity of $L_{\text{jet}} \sim 10^{44}$ erg/s concentrated within $r \sim 10 r_g \sim 6 \times 10^{12}$ cm:

$$\rho_{\text{jet}} \sim \frac{L_{\text{jet}}}{4\pi r^2 c^3} \sim \frac{10^{44}}{4\pi (6 \times 10^{12})^2 (3 \times 10^{10})^3} \sim 10^{-14} \text{ g/cm}^3$$

This is far too tenuous to gravitationally accelerate a PBH. The jet cannot entrain PBHs.

**Rating: 1/10** — The gravitational coupling is too weak.

#### Mechanism 5: Tidal Disruption of a PBH Cluster
If a dense PBH sub-cluster (bound by their mutual gravity and possibly embedded in a DM minihalo) falls toward Sgr A*, tidal forces will strip PBHs from the cluster. The tidal radius:

$$r_t = r \left(\frac{M_{\text{cluster}}}{3 M_{\text{Sgr A*}}}\right)^{1/3}$$

For a cluster of $N = 10^6$ PBHs each of mass $10^{20}$ g ($M_{\text{cluster}} = 10^{26}$ g $\sim 5 \times 10^{-8} M_\odot$) at $r = 1$ pc:

$$r_t = 3 \times 10^{18} \text{ cm} \times \left(\frac{5 \times 10^{-8}}{1.2 \times 10^{7}}\right)^{1/3} \approx 3 \times 10^{18} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-5} \approx 5 \times 10^{13} \text{ cm} \approx 3 \text{ AU}$$

Stripped PBHs would have velocities characteristic of the cluster's orbital velocity at the tidal radius, which at $r \sim 0.01$ pc from Sgr A* is $\sim 0.014c$. This produces a "spray" of PBHs, but not at relativistic speeds.

**Rating: 4/10** — Produces a dispersed population, but not a high-velocity "shotgun."

#### Summary of Ejection Mechanisms

| Mechanism | Max Achievable $v/c$ | Feasibility | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Three-body slingshot | 0.1 - 0.5 | Moderate | Common in dense environments |
| GW recoil kick | 0.01 - 0.1 | Moderate | Well-established, but too slow |
| Penrose/BSW process | 0.99+ ($\gamma \sim 10-100$) | Low-Moderate | Requires $a^* > 0.998$ |
| Jet entrainment | N/A | Very Low | Gravitational coupling too weak |
| Tidal stripping | 0.01 - 0.05 | High | Produces spray, not relativistic |

**Combined Feasibility Rating for Sub-Claim C: 4/10** — Some mechanisms can eject PBHs, but reaching $>0.99c$ requires the extreme Penrose/BSW process with unrealistic fine-tuning.

**Revised physically plausible scenario:** A "shotgun" of PBHs ejected at **0.01c - 0.1c** via three-body interactions and tidal stripping from a dense central PBH cluster. This is far more conservative than the stated $>0.99c$ but is physically well-motivated.

### 2.4 Sub-Claim D: Effects on the Solar System

This is where the hypothesis becomes **most physically concrete and testable**. Let's compute the observable effects of a PBH transit through the solar system.

#### Transit Rate

Assuming PBHs constitute a fraction $f_{\text{PBH}}$ of the local dark matter density:

$$\Gamma_{\text{transit}} = n_{\text{PBH}} \cdot \sigma_{\text{target}} \cdot v_{\text{PBH}}$$

where $n_{\text{PBH}} = f_{\text{PBH}} \rho_{\text{DM}} / M_{\text{PBH}}$.

Local DM density: $\rho_{\text{DM}} \approx 0.3 \text{ GeV/cm}^3 \approx 5 \times 10^{-25}$ g/cm$^3$.

For $M_{\text{PBH}} = 10^{20}$ g and $f_{\text{PBH}} = 1$:

$$n_{\text{PBH}} = \frac{5 \times 10^{-25}}{10^{20}} = 5 \times 10^{-45} \text{ cm}^{-3}$$

Cross-section of the inner solar system (sphere of radius 5 AU):

$$\sigma = \pi (5 \times 1.5 \times 10^{13})^2 = \pi \times 5.6 \times 10^{27} \approx 1.8 \times 10^{28} \text{ cm}^2$$

Transit velocity: $v \sim 2 \times 10^7$ cm/s (galactic virial velocity).

$$\Gamma = 5 \times 10^{-45} \times 1.8 \times 10^{28} \times 2 \times 10^7 \approx 1.8 \times 10^{-9} \text{ s}^{-1} \approx 0.06 \text{ yr}^{-1}$$

**A PBH passes through the inner solar system roughly once every ~17 years** (if they are all of DM at $10^{20}$ g). This is consistent with the UC Santa Cruz (September 2024) estimate of approximately one transit per decade.

For Earth specifically:

$$\sigma_{\text{Earth}} = \pi R_\oplus^2 = \pi (6.4 \times 10^8)^2 \approx 1.3 \times 10^{18} \text{ cm}^2$$

$$\Gamma_{\text{Earth}} = 5 \times 10^{-45} \times 1.3 \times 10^{18} \times 2 \times 10^7 \approx 1.3 \times 10^{-19} \text{ s}^{-1} \approx 4 \times 10^{-12} \text{ yr}^{-1}$$

**A PBH hits Earth once every $\sim 2.5 \times 10^{11}$ years** — far longer than the age of the universe. Direct Earth transit of a single ambient PBH is exceedingly rare.

**However:** The H36 hypothesis posits a **swarm** of ejected PBHs, dramatically increasing the local number density during the swarm's passage. If a swarm of $N = 10^6$ PBHs passes through a volume $V \sim (100 \text{ AU})^3$ over a transit time $\Delta t \sim 1$ year:

$$n_{\text{swarm}} = \frac{N}{V} = \frac{10^6}{(1.5 \times 10^{15})^3} = \frac{10^6}{3.4 \times 10^{45}} \approx 3 \times 10^{-40} \text{ cm}^{-3}$$

This is $\sim 6 \times 10^4$ times higher than the ambient DM-PBH density, and would produce:
- ~1000 transits through the inner solar system during the swarm passage
- ~$10^{-8}$ probability of a direct Earth hit during a single swarm event

#### Energy Deposition: PBH Through a Planet

Using the gravitational wake energy deposition formula (Ostriker 1999):

$$\frac{dE}{dx} = \frac{4\pi (G M_{\text{PBH}})^2 \rho_{\text{medium}}}{v^2} \ln\Lambda$$

For a PBH ($M = 10^{20}$ g) passing through Earth ($\rho \sim 5.5$ g/cm$^3$) at $v = 200$ km/s:

$$\frac{dE}{dx} = \frac{4\pi (6.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 10^{20})^2 \times 5.5}{(2 \times 10^7)^2} \times 30$$

$$= \frac{4\pi \times 4.45 \times 10^{25} \times 5.5}{4 \times 10^{14}} \times 30$$

$$= \frac{4\pi \times 2.45 \times 10^{26}}{4 \times 10^{14}} \times 30 \approx 5.8 \times 10^{13} \text{ erg/cm}$$

Over a path length of $L \sim 1.3 \times 10^9$ cm (Earth diameter):

$$E_{\text{total}} \sim 5.8 \times 10^{13} \times 1.3 \times 10^9 = 7.5 \times 10^{22} \text{ erg} = 7.5 \times 10^{15} \text{ J}$$

This is comparable to a **magnitude 7 earthquake** ($\sim 2 \times 10^{15}$ J) or a **small nuclear weapon** (15 kt TNT $\approx 6.3 \times 10^{13}$ J — actually our estimate is ~100× larger). This would produce detectable seismic signatures.

For a PBH at **relativistic speeds** ($v = 0.5c$):

$$\frac{dE}{dx} \propto \frac{1}{v^2} \implies E_{\text{relativistic}} \sim E_{\text{virial}} \times \left(\frac{v_{\text{virial}}}{v_{\text{rel}}}\right)^2 = 7.5 \times 10^{22} \times \left(\frac{200}{1.5 \times 10^5}\right)^2 \approx 1.3 \times 10^{17} \text{ erg}$$

**Paradoxically, faster PBHs deposit LESS energy** (because they spend less time in the medium, and the wake has less time to develop). A relativistic PBH passes through Earth almost invisibly.

#### PBH Through the Sun

The Sun has much higher density in its core ($\rho_{\text{core}} \sim 150$ g/cm$^3$) and a path length of $L \sim 1.4 \times 10^{11}$ cm:

$$E_{\text{Sun}} \sim \frac{4\pi (GM)^2 \bar{\rho}}{v^2} L \ln\Lambda \sim \frac{4\pi \times 4.45 \times 10^{25} \times 70}{4 \times 10^{14}} \times 1.4 \times 10^{11} \times 30$$

$$\approx 1.3 \times 10^{27} \text{ erg} = 1.3 \times 10^{20} \text{ J}$$

The Sun's luminosity is $L_\odot = 3.8 \times 10^{33}$ erg/s, so this energy deposit equals **0.03 milliseconds** of solar output. Completely negligible for the Sun. The "anomalous core heating" described in H36 would not be observable for a single PBH transit.

**However**, for a swarm of $10^6$ PBHs, the cumulative energy is $\sim 10^{33}$ erg $\sim 0.3$ seconds of solar luminosity — still negligible, but potentially detectable via neutrino observatories if the energy is deposited in a burst pattern.

#### Orbital Perturbations ("Jitters")

A PBH of mass $M_{\text{PBH}} = 10^{20}$ g passing at closest approach distance $b$ from a planet of mass $M_p$ imparts a velocity kick:

$$\Delta v = \frac{2 G M_{\text{PBH}}}{b \cdot v_{\text{rel}}}$$

For $b = 1$ AU ($1.5 \times 10^{13}$ cm) and $v_{\text{rel}} = 200$ km/s:

$$\Delta v = \frac{2 \times 6.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 10^{20}}{1.5 \times 10^{13} \times 2 \times 10^7} = \frac{1.33 \times 10^{13}}{3 \times 10^{20}} \approx 4.4 \times 10^{-8} \text{ cm/s}$$

Earth's orbital velocity is $\sim 3 \times 10^6$ cm/s, so this perturbation is:

$$\frac{\Delta v}{v_{\oplus}} \sim 1.5 \times 10^{-14}$$

**Completely undetectable** with current astrometry. Even for a close approach of $b = 10^9$ cm ($\sim 0.07 R_\oplus$), the kick is only $\sim 0.7$ cm/s — detectable only with ultra-precise ranging (Mars would show $\sim 1$ meter displacement, consistent with the UC Santa Cruz 2024 estimate).

#### Microlensing Signatures

The Einstein crossing time for an asteroid-mass PBH:

$$t_E = \frac{r_E}{v_\perp} = \frac{\sqrt{4GM D_L D_{LS} / (c^2 D_S)}}{v_\perp}$$

For a PBH of $M = 10^{20}$ g $= 5 \times 10^{-14} M_\odot$ at $D_L = 1$ kpc, $D_S = 8$ kpc:

$$r_E = \sqrt{\frac{4 \times 6.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 10^{20} \times 3 \times 10^{21} \times 2.16 \times 10^{22}}{9 \times 10^{20} \times 2.5 \times 10^{22}}} \approx 2.8 \times 10^{9} \text{ cm}$$

$$t_E = \frac{2.8 \times 10^9}{2 \times 10^7} = 140 \text{ s} \approx 2.3 \text{ minutes}$$

**Extremely short timescale** — this is why HSC/Subaru struggles to detect asteroid-mass PBHs. A swarm passing through the line of sight to background stars would produce a **burst of ultra-short microlensing events**, potentially detectable with high-cadence surveys (Rubin Observatory LSST, Roman Space Telescope).

## Part III: The "Shotgun" Geometry — Mathematical Model

### 3.1 Swarm Propagation

Model the ejected PBH swarm as a conical spray with opening half-angle $\theta$ and initial velocity $v_0$. After traveling a distance $d$ from the galactic center:

$$R_{\text{cone}}(d) = d \tan\theta$$

For $\theta = 5°$ and $d = 8.2$ kpc (distance to Sgr A*):

$$R_{\text{cone}} = 8.2 \text{ kpc} \times \tan(5°) \approx 0.72 \text{ kpc} = 2350 \text{ ly}$$

The cross-sectional area of the swarm at the Solar System's distance:

$$A_{\text{swarm}} = \pi R_{\text{cone}}^2 = \pi (0.72 \text{ kpc})^2 \approx 1.63 \text{ kpc}^2$$

The probability of the Solar System being inside the swarm cone depends on the fraction of the sky covered:

$$P = \frac{A_{\text{swarm}}}{4\pi d^2} = \frac{\pi (d \tan\theta)^2}{4\pi d^2} = \frac{\tan^2\theta}{4} \approx 1.9 \times 10^{-3}$$

For a narrow jet-like ejection ($\theta = 1°$): $P \approx 7.6 \times 10^{-5}$ — a ~0.008% chance.

### 3.2 Arrival Time Dispersion

PBHs ejected with a velocity distribution $v \in [v_{\min}, v_{\max}]$ arrive at the Solar System over a time interval:

$$\Delta t_{\text{arrival}} = \frac{d}{v_{\min}} - \frac{d}{v_{\max}}$$

For $v_{\min} = 0.01c$, $v_{\max} = 0.1c$, $d = 8.2$ kpc $= 2.53 \times 10^{22}$ cm:

$$t_{\text{fast}} = \frac{2.53 \times 10^{22}}{3 \times 10^9} = 8.4 \times 10^{12} \text{ s} \approx 267,000 \text{ yr}$$

$$t_{\text{slow}} = \frac{2.53 \times 10^{22}}{3 \times 10^8} = 8.4 \times 10^{13} \text{ s} \approx 2,670,000 \text{ yr}$$

$$\Delta t_{\text{arrival}} \approx 2.4 \text{ Myr}$$

The swarm arrives as a **protracted rain lasting millions of years**, not a single burst. This dramatically reduces the instantaneous density enhancement.

### 3.3 Deflection by Galactic Magnetic Fields

PBHs are electrically neutral. Unlike cosmic rays, they are **not deflected by magnetic fields**. Their trajectories are purely gravitational, following geodesics through the galactic potential. This means:

1. No magnetic deflection or scattering — the swarm maintains its collimation
2. Gravitational deflection by intervening masses (stars, molecular clouds) is negligible for $v > 0.01c$
3. **The PBH swarm is a true "ballistic" projectile** — its trajectory is predictable from Sgr A* to the Solar System

This is a **key advantage** over cosmic ray "shockwaves" (H1, H4) which are diffusively scattered by the galactic magnetic field.

## Part VI: Expanded Mathematical Framework for Simulation

### 6.1 PBH N-Body Integration

Each PBH in the swarm is tracked as a point mass following a geodesic in the combined Sgr A* + Galactic + Solar System potential:

$$\ddot{\vec{r}}_i = -\nabla\Phi_{\text{gal}}(\vec{r}_i) - \sum_{j \neq i} \frac{G M_j (\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_j)}{|\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_j|^3} - \sum_k \frac{G M_k (\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_k)}{|\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_k|^3}$$

where $j$ runs over other PBHs (mutual gravity) and $k$ runs over solar system bodies.

For the "shotgun" scenario with $N \sim 10^3 - 10^6$ PBHs at $M \sim 10^{20}$ g, mutual gravitational interactions between PBHs are negligible:

$$\frac{GM_{\text{PBH}}}{r_{\text{PBH-PBH}}^2} \ll \frac{GM_\odot}{r_{\text{PBH-Sun}}^2}$$

Simplifying to independent test particles in the solar system potential:

$$\ddot{\vec{r}}_i = -\sum_k \frac{G M_k (\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_k)}{|\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_k|^3}$$

### 6.2 Gravitational Perturbation on Planets

The perturbation on planet $k$ from PBH $i$:

$$\delta\ddot{\vec{r}}_k = -\frac{G M_{\text{PBH},i} (\vec{r}_k - \vec{r}_i)}{|\vec{r}_k - \vec{r}_i|^3}$$

This is a standard perturber term in the N-body equations. The perturbation is strongest at closest approach:

$$|\delta\ddot{\vec{r}}_k|_{\max} = \frac{G M_{\text{PBH}}}{b_{\min}^2}$$

For $M_{\text{PBH}} = 10^{20}$ g and $b_{\min} = 0.1$ AU:

$$|\delta\ddot{\vec{r}}_k|_{\max} = \frac{6.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 10^{20}}{(1.5 \times 10^{12})^2} \approx 3 \times 10^{-12} \text{ cm/s}^2$$

Solar gravity at 1 AU: $g_\odot = 0.6$ cm/s$^2$. Perturbation ratio: $\sim 5 \times 10^{-12}$ — deeply sub-detectable in a simulation context, but physically real.

### 6.3 Microlensing Rendering

For background stars at distance $D_S$, a PBH at distance $D_L$ (within the solar system, $D_L \sim 1-100$ AU $\ll D_S$) creates a magnification:

$$A(u) = \frac{u^2 + 2}{u\sqrt{u^2 + 4}}$$

where $u = \theta / \theta_E$ is the angular separation in units of the Einstein angle:

$$\theta_E = \sqrt{\frac{4GM_{\text{PBH}}}{c^2 D_L}} \approx 2.5 \times 10^{-4} \text{ arcsec} \times \sqrt{\frac{M_{\text{PBH}}}{10^{20} \text{ g}} \cdot \frac{1 \text{ AU}}{D_L}}$$

For a PBH at 10 AU, $\theta_E \approx 8 \times 10^{-5}$ arcsec. This is far below the angular resolution of the simulation's rendered starfield, so the visual effect would be modeled as a **transient brightness amplification** of background stars rather than a resolved image distortion.

The observable signature: brief ($\sim$ seconds) brightening of individual background stars as the PBH swarm passes between the camera and the starfield.

## Part VII: The Deep Brainstorm — Novel Extensions

### 7.1 "Hawking Firefly" Effect

PBHs in the mass range $M \sim 10^{15} - 10^{17}$ g are **actively evaporating via Hawking radiation**. Their temperature:

$$T_H = \frac{\hbar c^3}{8\pi G M k_B} \approx 1.2 \times 10^{13} \text{ K} \times \left(\frac{10^{15} \text{ g}}{M}\right)$$

At $M = 10^{16}$ g: $T_H \approx 1.2 \times 10^{12}$ K — emitting gamma rays at $E \sim k_B T \sim 100$ MeV.

Luminosity:
$$L_H = \frac{\hbar c^6}{15360 \pi G^2 M^2} \approx 3.6 \times 10^{19} \text{ erg/s} \times \left(\frac{10^{15} \text{ g}}{M}\right)^2$$

At $M = 10^{16}$ g: $L_H \approx 3.6 \times 10^{17}$ erg/s $\approx 3.6 \times 10^{10}$ W.

**A swarm of evaporating PBHs would appear as a constellation of faint, moving gamma-ray point sources** — "Hawking fireflies" — detectable by Fermi-LAT or future gamma-ray telescopes.

This adds a **dramatic visual element** to the simulation: invisible gravitational bullets that occasionally flicker with gamma-ray Hawking emission.

### 7.2 "Gravitational Raindrops" — Acoustic Analogy

The passage of each PBH through the heliosphere creates a gravitational "ripple" in the solar wind plasma. The perturbation:

$$\delta\rho / \rho_0 \sim \frac{GM_{\text{PBH}}}{b \cdot v^2} \times \frac{v}{c_s}$$

For a PBH at $b = 1$ AU in the solar wind ($\rho_0 \sim 5 \times 10^{-24}$ g/cm$^3$, $c_s \sim 50$ km/s, $v = 200$ km/s):

$$\delta\rho / \rho_0 \sim \frac{6.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 10^{20}}{1.5 \times 10^{13} \times (2 \times 10^7)^2} \times \frac{2 \times 10^7}{5 \times 10^6} \sim 4.4 \times 10^{-14}$$

Negligible. But the **Mach cone** wake (at $\mathcal{M} = v/c_s = 4$) creates a beautiful geometric structure — a conical density enhancement trailing each PBH through the solar wind. In the simulation, this could be rendered as a faint, expanding Mach cone visible in the heliospheric density visualization.

### 7.3 "Binary PBH Gravitational Wave Chirps"

If the ejected swarm contains PBH binaries (which form naturally in dense environments), these binaries emit gravitational waves as they inspiral:

$$f_{\text{GW}} = \frac{1}{\pi} \sqrt{\frac{G(M_1 + M_2)}{4a^3}}$$

For two $10^{20}$ g PBHs separated by $a = 10^5$ cm (1 km):

$$f_{\text{GW}} = \frac{1}{\pi} \sqrt{\frac{6.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 2 \times 10^{20}}{4 \times 10^{15}}} \approx 0.6 \text{ Hz}$$

This falls in the **decihertz band** — between LISA (mHz) and LIGO (Hz-kHz). Future detectors like DECIGO could detect such binaries if they pass close to the solar system.

The merger time:
$$\tau = \frac{5c^5 a^4}{256 G^3 M_1 M_2 (M_1 + M_2)} \approx \frac{5 \times (3 \times 10^{10})^5 \times 10^{20}}{256 \times (6.67 \times 10^{-8})^3 \times (10^{20})^2 \times 2 \times 10^{20}}$$

$$\approx 1.2 \times 10^{71} / 3.8 \times 10^{19} \approx 3.2 \times 10^{51} \text{ s}$$

Far longer than the age of the universe. PBH binaries at this mass and separation do NOT merge. However, if the binary is **much tighter** ($a \sim 1$ cm), the merger time drops dramatically. Such tight binaries would merge during transit, producing a **localized gravitational wave burst within the solar system** — a truly exotic signature.
